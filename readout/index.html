Rates and card delinquency: the two-year lag is one recession, not a rule

The finding

Run the standard analysis on the full sample and you get a clean, quotable answer: changes in the fed funds rate lead changes in credit-card delinquency by eight quarters, correlation +0.42. It looks like a planning parameter.

It is not one. Twelve quarters around the 2008–10 financial crisis — 13% of the sample — supply 65% of the covariance behind that number. Remove those twelve quarters and the eight-quarter correlation falls from +0.42 to −0.07. The lead does not weaken; it disappears. What the full sample is describing is one recession, and the two-year gap is the distance between the 2004–06 hiking cycle and the unemployment shock that actually drove the defaults.

Split the sample and the two halves contradict each other outright. On 2000–2019 the lead is 9 quarters (r = +0.71). On 2010–2026 it is 2 quarters (r = +0.56) — essentially contemporaneous. At a nine-quarter lead the two windows disagree about the sign: +0.71 against −0.35. A team that fitted the full-sample rule and applied it to the current cycle would have had the direction wrong, not just the timing.

The mechanism is visible in the data. Unemployment moves with card delinquency at a zero-quarter lag in every pre-2020 window (r = +0.58 on 2000–2019) and more tightly than the policy rate does. In 2008 the rate was an early and coincidental marker of a labour-market collapse it did not cause. Since 2010, with no such shock in the sample, what remains is the fast channel — card APRs are indexed to prime, so a policy move reaches a household's minimum payment within a billing cycle or two, and delinquency responds inside a couple of quarters.

Results

Both series were pulled from FRED and aligned to quarters: FEDFUNDS is monthly and was averaged into each quarter (not sampled — a quarter's rate is the mean of its three months), DRCCLACBS is already quarterly, and the two were inner-joined, leaving 106 complete quarters from 2000 Q1 to 2026 Q2 with no gaps, no duplicates and no missing values.

Correlating the levels is not available: an ADF test cannot reject a unit root in the delinquency series (p = 0.52), so the +0.21 level correlation is spurious. All results below use year-over-year (four-quarter) changes, which are stationary for both series (p = 0.003 and p = 0.030) and are also the form a risk committee reads. A first-difference transform gives the same shape (peak at 9 quarters), so the conclusion is not an artefact of the transform.

Two robustness checks. Leave-one-quarter-out on the full-sample eight-quarter correlation moves it only between +0.365 and +0.438 — so the result is not a single outlier quarter, it is a whole episode, which is the harder problem. And measured cycle by cycle, peak year-over-year rate rise to peak year-over-year delinquency rise, the three hiking cycles in the sample give three different answers: 2004–06, +14 quarters; 2015–18, −6 quarters (delinquency turned first); 2022–23, +1 quarter.

Where the current cycle stands: fed funds is 3.63% and falling (−0.70pp year over year), card delinquency is 2.85%, down from a cycle peak of 3.22% in 2024 Q2 and below the 2000–2026 average of 3.42%.

What follows for a risk team

Do not carry a single lag number in a model or a policy document. The honest parameter is a range that moves with the regime: 1–2 quarters in the current one, up to 9 in a sample dominated by a credit crisis. If a scorecard, stress overlay or ECL model has a fixed rate-to-delinquency lag baked in, find out which window it was fitted on and when — a coefficient fitted on 2000–2019 is pointing backwards in this cycle.

Watch unemployment, not the policy rate, as the leading indicator. Rates lead delinquency only when they precede a labour-market shock, and then it is the shock doing the work. Unemployment is contemporaneous with delinquency and strictly more informative. A rate-based early-warning trigger is really an untested bet that a downturn follows the hikes.

Size the rate channel from the current regime, and expect it to over-predict at large impulses. In the 2010–2026 window 100bp of policy maps to about 30bp of delinquency at a two-quarter lead, explaining roughly a third of the variance. Applied to this cycle that rule overshoots: the peak year-over-year rate impulse of +4.40pp implies +1.30pp of year-over-year delinquency against +0.92pp observed. Delinquency did rise 1.69pp from its 2021 Q3 trough to its 2024 Q2 peak, but part of that was normalisation from a pandemic low held down by fiscal transfers. A linear rate coefficient is a serviceable first-order term and a poor stress parameter — at large impulses it reads high.

Re-estimate on a rolling window, and publish the instability. This relationship changed twice in twenty-six years. Any model that assumes it is fixed will look accurate in backtest and fail in the next regime. Re-fit on a rolling window, monitor whether the peak lead has moved, and report the range rather than the point estimate — the honest deliverable to a risk committee is the fact that this parameter is unstable, not a false precision about where it currently sits.

One caveat on scope. This is two public aggregate series for all commercial banks. It says nothing about a specific book's mix, vintage, credit tier or underwriting, and a subprime-weighted portfolio may well have a different and faster response. Treat it as a prior about the macro channel and a caution about fixed lag assumptions — not as a substitute for fitting the relationship on our own portfolio.
